By the night of Day 1, I had hundreds of pieces of content and no idea which ones went together. **The fix wasn't a better layout. It was labelling every piece before anyone, me or an agent, built a page out of them.**

## Hundreds of pieces, and nobody could see them

By evening the hackathon had produced 201 written pieces, 204 video items, 24 reusable skills, 94 LinkedIn posts, and the day's recordings cut into 127 moments. None of it was listed anywhere. So the pages stacked the wrong things, and a lot of the decisions about what went where were silly.

What I wanted was simple: list and describe every piece, so it's legible to me and to the agents helping me put it together. On camera that night I put it more bluntly: label everything, make it legible, and let a bunch of agents work out which 20 of these hundred pieces actually matter.

## Taste is the one label only I can give

So I started there. I sat at a little grading page and watched every clip, one at a time, with no AI opinion on screen. 126 clips in about an hour, mostly at 1.5x: 44 Great, 28 Yes, 2 Maybe, 52 No.

A grade isn't a promise. Yes or Great just means it's acceptable, and Greats win ties over Yeses. (I had to spell that out at 21:24, because one agent had read every Great as must-publish and another had started writing an entry for each one.)

## Then every keeper got a side card

**Every clip I kept got a side card: 74 of them.** A card is a short label an agent reads instead of watching the video: the gist, my exact words (a script rejected any quote that wasn't word for word), 1 to 4 themes from one shared list of 14, related pieces with the kind of link (leads into, pairs with, repeats, contradicts), what it could serve as, and watch-outs.

[figure: the side card for the 13:20 moment I closed every session by accident, with its gist, themes, links, uses and watch-outs | legibility/cards/day1-3.md]

Take the worst moment of my day. At 13:20 I closed every AI session building the site, by accident: "So that was bad." Its card tags it failure and recovery, personality, build in public. It leads into me asking a fresh session to bring them all back, then into 13:44, when the recovery became a reusable skill. Watch-out: needs one line explaining what the sessions are.

The shared list is what makes it work. Personalisation at scale means the same thing on the first card and the 74th, so an agent can sort by it, count it and see what's missing.

[figure: the 14-theme vocabulary every card draws from, and which cards carry each theme | legibility/cards/INDEX.md]

The cards were honest about me, too. One caught me claiming six months of prior work in two clips and about nine months in another. And I didn't type a single note while grading, so every *could serve as* is an agent's call, not mine. That's a real gap.

Then every usable piece, clips included, went into one catalog, 178 rows in all: what it is, its themes, the jobs it can do in a story (hook, proof, thesis, method, closer) and who it serves most.

[figure: a few catalog rows with their themes, jobs and who each one serves | orchestration/local/catalog/LEGOS.md]

## Same library, six different paths

Then agents could compose. The site has a page for each of six people at Addvery: the CEO, the head of growth, two cofounders, the delivery lead and the BI lead. **Nobody wrote six sites. The labels did the routing.**

The BI lead gets every data-handling moment I have: API keys set up off camera, the leak check before a job runs, every public photo cited to its source. The CEO's page leads on the money: "if it's not lowering their labor costs, then it has to be increasing their revenue." Growth gets personalisation against volume. Delivery gets the bottleneck and the recovery. Same pieces, a different story from each seat.

[figure: one reader's path, the BI lead's set of my lines, each with the clip it's from and why it matters to that role | orchestration/local/catalog/QUOTES-BY-PERSON.md]

That's what I'd promised at 11:17 that morning: "the agent's going to look at which transcripts are going to match which stakeholder." The labels made that sentence true.

They also showed me a hole. ICP filtering (sorting leads into ideal customer profiles) is the thing I most wanted to show off, and it had three moments, only one of them Great. Better to learn that the night before than after the links went out.

How three agents then argued for whole versions of the site is its own piece: [Best of three, instead of redlining everything one by one](/demos/best-of-three-instead-of-redlining).

## This is account-based personalisation, run by agents

Outbound used to be "two things that you had to balance, personalization and volume." Now "you can do both."

The expensive part isn't writing the pages. It's knowing what you've got. **Label your content library once, and an agent can build a page per stakeholder, per account.** I said it in my intro video: "what if every single person in your CRM, like your key accounts, could have this level of personalization."

That same morning I admitted "I cannot do this to 30 companies a day yet." The labelling is the yet. You pay for it once, and every page after that gets cheap. Picture your conversion rate if every account in your CRM got a page like this. It's possible, and the ROI would be unbelievable.

None of you is going to read all of this, and I know it. That's why each of the six gets a path. The labels already know which one.
