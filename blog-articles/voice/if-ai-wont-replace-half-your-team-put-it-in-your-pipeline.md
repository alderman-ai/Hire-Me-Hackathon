GTM engineers are the solution to the biggest social question of the AI era.

A lot of companies are saying some version of this: "We want to bring an AI in, but we're not going to fire half the team." Okay, if you say so. But AI only saves you money if it reduces some costs, and the cost it mostly reduces is labor.

So if it's not lowering labor costs, it has to be increasing revenue. **That means GTM engineers who focus AI on the pipeline are the ones helping companies keep their promise:** that AI is a way to do more business and grow the whole team together, not to replace half of it.

That's my stance as a GTM engineer. I've been a freelancer for 10 years, coming from marketing, and I've spent about nine months on an AI sabbatical doing nothing but building in Claude Code. Now I want to take what I've learned into a real company, with a real CRM and real IT restrictions, and actually get the satisfaction of making numbers go up.

> [!summary]
> - If AI isn't lowering labor costs, it has to be increasing revenue.
> - Put AI in your pipeline first, so your people get more done while you bring in more work.
> - Outreach no longer has to pick between personalization and volume. Now you can do both.
> - AI isn't free, so after the first build someone has to keep making it cheaper and better.

## Keeping everyone takes the biggest saving off the table

For AI to save a company any money, there has to be some return on that investment. Promise not to replace half your staff with robots, and you've just taken the biggest saving off the table. That's fine. It just means the return has to come from somewhere else.

> [!quote]
> The only way to get the leverage of AI and not need to drop half your team is if you're also leveraging the amount of new work you're getting.

## What a GTM engineer actually builds

You have people on your team, I guarantee it, already playing with Claude Code and building agentic workflows. But what happens when you have someone who's really good at that? I come from marketing, the standard path for a GTM engineer, and the job is basically workflows that touch your pipeline.

To show what that means, I spent 12 hours building live on camera, hackathon style. One build was a prospect research pipeline. It builds an org chart and a profile of each key decision maker, looks for anyone who's been there less than six months, and digs through the Wayback Machine and the job portals for the advert they answered, if it's still out there somewhere.

A lot of roles are fuzzy on responsibilities, and the advert sometimes gives the 90-day goals. If someone is in month four and you have their 90-day goals, that's gold for crafting custom messaging. (You should be doing this to your own targets too.)

The other build was a lead flow in GetSales, the outreach tool:

1. Leads come out of Sales Navigator as a CSV.
2. The flow pulls in company research.
3. One custom first message is worked out before import, as its own variable.
4. A classifier would decide which workflow each lead goes into.

We landed on 36 workflows: four main ICPs (ideal customer profiles), three languages and three tactics. It was built to be legible more than functional, and no real data ran through it. ## You don't have to pick between personalization and volume

Before AI, reaching out to people meant balancing two things: personalization and volume. You could do heavy research, but that takes a lot of time, so you'd be limited to your key accounts. Or you could shotgun blast lots of people. Most people ended up in the middle or the shotgun blast, and at the time, shotgun blast was actually correct.

What's new is that you don't have to pick anymore. Now you can do both. **And this is not known yet.** That's the knowledge arbitrage: the value of knowing something before other people do.

My 12 hours on camera sat at the costly end. I can't do this for 30 companies a day yet.

> [!quote]
> But once it's automated, it looks costly, and it isn't. Everyone's blasting out AI, low quality, high volume, and there's going to be a space for so good AI that it looks human and looks impossible for it to be AI.
> Alex Alderman, on camera during the hackathon, 2 Nov 2026

That's the niche. So good they think you spent three days building it just for them, when an agent was running in the background for a couple of hours. Now picture your top 100 accounts getting that. Would it be valuable? **That's new work coming in, with nobody cut.**

## People stay the bottleneck

Keeping the whole team isn't charity, either. You can have 20 agents running side by side, so time is no longer the bottleneck. The bottleneck is focus, judgment and discernment.

> [!quote]
> The bottleneck is always, always, always human. It's not the amount of stuff that the agents can focus on, it's humans.
> Alex Alderman, on camera during the hackathon, 2 Nov 2026

Humans have RAM too. There's a human context window. Either you decide what you're offloading to the AI, or you keep the velocity at a certain level, and you buy a little more room by writing good guiding documents at the start. [Alex: is this true? This is why the people stay: they set the limit on how much the AI can do]

## AI isn't free, and someone has to keep upgrading it

I was working at an AI research and development company when ChatGPT came out, and I used it daily to try to get some ROI on the time I put in. For years, that meant spending hours to save 15 minutes. Hopefully the ROI is here now. What's different is [Alex: what changed that makes the ROI real now].

It's still not free. It costs tokens, and a first build made in 12 hours under time pressure starts on the expensive side. You might ask: if it's all built in the first month, why do you even need someone after that? Because it needs to work better. It needs to get cheaper. People need to learn how to use it, and it needs upgrading, constantly.

So if your company has made the promise, put AI in your pipeline first, and make sure someone owns it after the first build. **That's the GTM engineer's job:** keep upgrading what's built, and help the company keep its promise. I'd like it to be mine.
