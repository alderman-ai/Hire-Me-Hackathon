GTM engineers are the solution to the biggest social question of the AI era.

A lot of companies are saying some version of this: "We want to bring an AI in, but we're not going to fire half the team." Okay, if you say so. But AI only saves you money if it reduces some costs, and the cost it's mostly going to reduce is labor.

So if it's not lowering labor costs, it has to be increasing revenue. **That means GTM engineers who focus AI on the pipeline are the ones helping companies keep their promise:** that AI is a way to do more business and have the whole team grow together, not to replace half of it.

That's my stance as a GTM engineer. I've been a freelancer for 10 years, coming from marketing, and spent about nine months on an AI sabbatical doing nothing but building in Claude Code. Now I want to take that into a real company and get the satisfaction of making numbers go up.

> [!summary]
> - If AI isn't lowering labor costs, it has to be increasing revenue.
> - Personalization and volume aren't a trade-off anymore.
> - Building is a phase. Refining is the main work.

## Keeping everyone takes the biggest saving off the table

Promise not to replace half your staff with robots, and the biggest saving is off the table. There still has to be some return on that investment.

> [!quote]
> The only way to get the leverage of AI and not need to drop half your team is if you're also leveraging the amount of new work you're getting.
> Alex Alderman, on camera during the hackathon, 2 Nov 2026

> [!diagram] flow
> How AI pays for itself without firing half the team
> 1. The promise: AI is not a way to fire half the company
> 2. There has to be some return on that investment
> 3. If it's not lowering labor costs, it has to be increasing revenue
> 4. GTM engineers focus the AI on the pipeline
> 5. More business, and the whole team grows together

That new work comes in through the pipeline.

## Put AI in your pipeline first

For the people who really want to save their team, and do AI the ethical way (whatever that means), the order matters.

> [!quote]
> The first place you need to put AI is in your pipeline, so that you can upskill your people to get more done and at the same time bring in more work. Otherwise you're going to have very uncomfortable decisions, and your comfort levels may vary.
> Alex Alderman, on camera during the hackathon, 2 Nov 2026

In a sales team, the whole information-gathering process is completely automatable, so you build the tools for the salespeople and teach them how to use them. The meeting prep alone is tons of hours people save on preparing pitches.

## What a GTM engineer actually builds

The job is basically workflows that touch your pipeline. To show what that means, I spent 12 hours building live on camera.

One build was a prospect research pipeline: an org chart, a profile of each key decision maker, and, for anyone who's been there less than six months, the job advert they answered, dug out of the Wayback Machine. The advert sometimes gives the 90-day goals.

> [!pull]
> If someone is in month four and you have their 90-day goals, that's gold for crafting custom messaging.

The other was 36 outreach workflows in GetSales: four main ICPs, three languages, three tactics. A classifier decides which lead goes where, and each lead's first message is worked out before import, as its own variable. It was built to be legible more than functional.

## You don't have to pick between personalization and volume

Before AI, reaching out meant balancing personalization and volume. Heavy research takes a lot of time, so you'd be limited to your key accounts. Or you could shotgun blast lots of people. At the time, the shotgun blast was actually correct.

What's new is that you don't have to pick anymore. **Now you can do both.** And this is not known yet.

[media: graphic | one line from personalization to volume: before AI you pick a point on it (key accounts at one end, the shotgun blast at the other, most people in the middle); now you get both ends at once | carries the old trade-off faster than the paragraph, and sets up why the 12 hours look costly | make it with: Claude Design]

What I'm doing on camera is costly. I can't do this to 30 companies a day yet. Once it's automated, it looks costly, and it isn't, right? Everyone's blasting out AI, low quality, high volume, and there's going to be a space for so good AI that it looks human and looks impossible for it to be AI.

That's the niche. My goal is something that costs one to three or four euros, about 98% automated, where anyone who gets it thinks they're one of your top one or two accounts. Imagine if you could make everyone in your CRM feel that way. **That's new work coming in, with nobody cut.**

## The bottleneck is always human

You can have 20 agents running side by side, so time is no longer the bottleneck. **The bottleneck is focus, judgment, discernment.**

> [!quote]
> The bottleneck is always, always, always human. It's not the amount of stuff that the agents can focus on, it's humans.
> Alex Alderman, on camera during the hackathon, 2 Nov 2026

[media: video | my clip from 16:58 on day one, "the bottleneck is always human" | the moment it stopped being theory for me, and the bridge from my own limits to a team's | make it with: existing asset]

Getting real people to actually use AI is the part you can't automate.

## AI isn't free, and someone has to keep upgrading it

I was at an AI research and development company when ChatGPT came out, and for years, using it daily meant spending hours to save 15 minutes. Two years ago, context windows were really small, like floppy disks. Now everything is automated for you. Hopefully the ROI is here now.

It's still not free. It costs tokens. So if it's all built in the first month, why do you need someone after? Because it needs to work better, it needs to get cheaper, people need to learn how to use it, and it needs upgrading, constantly.

That means looking at the data and the outreach metrics. This approach is doing great, so lean harder into it. That one's not good, so cut it. Building is a phase, not the deliverable. **Refining is the main work.**

So put AI in your pipeline first, and make sure someone owns it after the first build. That's the GTM engineer's job: keep refining what's built, help the company keep its promise, and get the satisfaction of making numbers go up.
