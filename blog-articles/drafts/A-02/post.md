GTM engineers are the solution to the biggest social question of the AI era. A lot of companies are saying some version of this: "We want to bring an AI in, but we're not going to fire half the team." Okay, if you say so, but AI only saves you money if it reduces some costs.

If it's not lowering labor costs, then it has to be increasing revenue. That means GTM engineers who focus AI on the pipeline are the ones helping companies keep their promise: that AI is a way to do more business and have their whole team grow together, rather than replace half their team.

That's my stance as a GTM engineer. I've been a freelancer for 10 years, coming from marketing, and I've spent about nine months on an AI sabbatical doing nothing but building in Claude Code. Now I'm looking for a place to apply what I've learned in a real company, with a real CRM and real IT restrictions, and actually get the satisfaction of making numbers go up.

> [!summary]
> - If AI isn't lowering labor costs, it has to be increasing revenue.
> - Put AI in your pipeline first, so your people get more done while you bring in more work.
> - You don't have to pick between personalization and volume: now you can do both.
> - AI isn't free, so after the first build someone has to keep making it cheaper and better.

## Keeping everyone takes the biggest saving off the table

For AI to save a company any money, there has to be some return on that investment, and the cost it's mostly going to reduce is labor. Promise not to replace half your staff with robots, and you've taken the biggest saving off the table.

> [!quote]
> The only way to get the leverage of AI and not need to drop half your team is if you're also leveraging the amount of new work you're getting.

## Put AI in your pipeline first

I think the GTM engineer is going to be one of the most in-demand positions over the next five, ten years, because a lot of companies are going to make that promise. The role that's going to be pretty important is boosting pipelines, and pipelines are always important.

For the people who really want to save their team, and really want to do AI the ethical way, whatever that means, the order matters.

> [!quote]

## A GTM engineer builds workflows that touch your pipeline

You have people on your team, I guarantee it, already playing with Claude Code and building agentic workflows. But what happens when you have someone who's really good at that? I come from a marketing background, the standard path for a GTM engineer, and the job is basically workflows that touch your pipeline.

To show what that means, I spent 12 hours building live on camera, in a hackathon format. One build was a prospect research pipeline: it builds an org chart and a profile of each key decision maker, finds anyone who's been there less than six months, and digs through the Wayback Machine and the job portals for the advert they applied to, if it's still there somewhere.

A lot of roles are fuzzy on responsibilities, and the advert sometimes gives the 90-day goals. If someone is in month four and you have their 90-day goals, that's gold for crafting custom messaging. You should be doing this to your own targets too.

The other build was a lead flow in GetSales, where the drip sequences run:

1. Leads come out of Sales Nav as a CSV.
2. The flow pulls in company research.
3. One custom first message is calculated before import, as its own variable.
4. A classifier decides which workflow each lead goes into.

We landed on 36 workflows: four main ICPs, three languages and three tactics. The demo was built to be legible more than functional, and no real data ran through it. On a real pipeline, workflows like these [Alex: a result you've seen from AI on a real pipeline, with a number if you have one].

## You don't have to pick between personalization and volume

Before AI, reaching out to people meant balancing two things: personalization and volume. You could do heavy research, but that takes a lot of time, so you'd be limited to your key accounts. Or you could shotgun blast lots of people. Most people ended up in the middle or the shotgun blast, and at the time, shotgun blast was actually correct.

What's new now is that you don't have to pick. Now you can do both. And this is not known yet. That's the knowledge arbitrage.

The 12-hour build on camera sat at the costly end: a website in one company's branding, with a section for each key stakeholder. I can't do this for 30 companies a day yet.

> [!quote]
> But once it's automated, it looks costly, and it isn't. Everyone's blasting out AI, low quality, high volume, and there's going to be a space for so good AI that it looks human and looks impossible for it to be AI.
> Alex Alderman, on camera during the hackathon, 2 Nov 2026

That's the niche. So good that they think you spent three days building it just for them, when an agent was running in the background for a couple hours.

Most of what I built that day was skills and workflows, so a second run for a new company would take half the time and half the stress. Now think of your top 100 accounts getting that much outreach effort. Would that be valuable?

## People stay the bottleneck

You can have 20 agents running side by side, so time is no longer the bottleneck. The bottleneck is focus, judgment and discernment.

Once you know how much you can run in parallel, it's hard to take the time to make sure everything's right first. But it's so much more time efficient to get the beginning done well, because mistakes cascade and they just snowball.

> [!quote]
> The bottleneck is always, always, always human. It's not the amount of stuff that the agents can focus on, it's humans.
> Alex Alderman, on camera during the hackathon, 2 Nov 2026

Humans have RAM too. There's a human context window. Either you decide what you're offloading to the AI, or you keep the velocity at a certain amount, and you get a little more room if you take the time to write good guiding documents at the beginning. ## AI isn't free, and someone has to keep upgrading it

I was working at an AI research and development company when ChatGPT came out, and I used it daily to try to get some ROI on the time I put in. For years, it meant spending hours to save 15 minutes. Hopefully the ROI is here now. What's different is [Alex: what changed that makes the ROI real now].

It's still not free: it costs tokens. A first build is an MVP of something that works, and it's probably going to start on the more expensive side, because a 12-hour build under time pressure isn't going to be the most efficient.

You might ask: if it's all built in the first month, why do you even need someone after that? Because it needs to work better. It needs to get cheaper, which means making it more token efficient. Then people need to be taught how to use it, and it needs upgrading, constantly.

If your company has made that promise to its team, put AI in your pipeline first. Then make sure someone owns it after the first build. That person is a GTM engineer: the one who keeps upgrading what's built, and helps the company keep its promise.
