# Approve the research plan for the whole project

## What this is

**About a 3-minute read. Please answer by about 14:40, so the findings are ready by 16:00.**

This is a research plan: four questions, plus three open-ended checks, that shape every demo in the project, not just one. The session that runs the project wants the answers by 16:00, before it writes the plans for the individual demos. Nothing has been researched yet. Nothing starts until you approve.

**What you're approving.** The four questions below, used word for word. They cover:
1. how to make the work easy to follow at a glance;
2. what employers want to see from a GTM engineer;
3. what the law and LinkedIn's terms allow when six named people appear on the site and go through a live outreach run;
4. whether a personalised job-application website lands well with the person it's for.

Nothing about Addvery itself is on it, because you're doing that research yourself.

**What happens when you tick the box.**
1. Seven research helpers start at once: one per question, plus three wildcards. Each wildcard looks for the biggest open question the four miss, from one angle: can the project be built in time, can it be delivered, will it be well received?
2. When all seven are done, one more helper reads every report. It pulls out what they agree on and settles where they disagree. It sends back what's still open as follow-up questions for you.
3. The findings are filed in the research folder with an index, and every demo reads them before its plan is written.

**If you want changes,** leave the box empty and write them in the box at the bottom. You can strike, add or rewrite a question, change the three wildcard angles, or mark a question "deep". Deep means a multi-agent research run: much more thorough, and much more costly.

## The plan, as it stands

**Who reads the findings:** every demo. That's the scraping demo, the GetSales outreach demo, the blog pipeline, the three blog posts, the Canva LinkedIn templates, the LinkedIn content demo, the website, the clip-to-website analyser and the Canva design video. Question 3 matters most to the scraping demo, the GetSales outreach demo and the website.

**Where the questions come from:** the project's goal (one live site that works as "a highly personalized outreach job application"), its rule that every demo should be easy to follow rather than fully functional, with no real data except the GetSales live run on the six stakeholders, and the definition of done you signed off.

**Flagged for you:** questions 2 and 4 serve the project's overall goal rather than one specific item in the definition of done. The closest items are "the site has a section for each of the six stakeholders" and "the site shows every deliverable and its status".

**Question 1. Making the work easy to follow.** How do the strongest GTM-engineer portfolios, hackathon demos and "job application as outreach" sites make the work legible to a non-technical decision maker in under a minute? Is there a converged pattern (short video, live build log, before/after, a "how it works" panel)? What makes such demos read as toys, or as noise?
*Serves:* a visitor can follow the scraping demo without explanation; the video of the live GetSales run; the LinkedIn content demo on the site; the site showing every deliverable and its status. *Sources:* web search and web pages.

**Question 2. What a GTM engineer is hired to show.** What do agencies and B2B companies hiring GTM engineers in 2026 look for: skills, tool stacks, proof of work? Which of the ten deliverables map to that, and what does a hiring manager expect that none of them shows?
*Serves:* the project's overall goal (flagged above). *Sources:* job posts and practitioner writing, not Addvery's.

**Question 3. Personal data on a public site.** What do GDPR and LinkedIn's terms allow when a project scrapes public information about six named people, publishes a section about each on a public website, and runs them through a live outbound sequence from the operator's own account? What's the converged safe practice for personalised outreach of this kind, and what should stay off the site?
*Serves:* the live GetSales run on the six stakeholders; the site's six stakeholder sections; nothing published showing what it shouldn't. *Sources:* regulator guidance, case law and LinkedIn's published terms.

**Question 4. Does the format land?** Is there evidence (case studies, recruiter and founder accounts) on how recipients react to a personalised website built as a job application? What tips it from impressive to creepy or self-indulgent, and what makes the recipient act on it?
*Serves:* the project's overall goal (flagged above). *Sources:* web search and web pages.

**Three wildcards**, one angle each: can it be built in time? Can it be delivered? Will it be well received?

**Then one summary** of all seven reports, with recommendations for every demo's plan, and an index entry for each report.
