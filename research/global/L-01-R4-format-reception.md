## Verdict

1. **Is there a converged answer on whether the format lands? Partly.** The record is consistent on two points:
   - A site or campaign aimed at one employer reliably wins attention, often from the most senior person there. Airbnb's CEO and CMO replied to Mufleh. Shopify's COO replied to Fedorin. Four of five targeted creative directors called Brownstein back.
   - That attention turns into a hire at the target employer much less reliably. Of seven well-documented cases, four ended in a hire at the target and three didn't. Even this ratio is inflated by survivorship.
   - The most consistent payoff is the portfolio effect. A public, targeted piece of work drew offers from other companies: about 80 interview offers for Epstein, and interest from several companies for Mufleh. [C]
2. **What tips it to creepy? The research converges.** Personalisation backfires when it rests on information the recipient didn't expect you to have, especially information that was:
   - collected covertly,
   - inferred rather than stated by them,
   - taken from outside its original context,
   - or personal rather than professional.

   It also backfires when you don't say why you know it or why it fits them, and when the recipient can't tell what you want or how to step away. Transparency and control lower perceived creepiness, and trust cues reverse the penalty. [A: Aguirre et al. 2015; White et al. 2008; Kim, Barasz & John 2019; Langer & König 2018]
3. **What tips it to self-indulgent? Practitioners converge, with no research behind it.** These read as being about the applicant, not the company:
   - stunts whose main content is the applicant's daring,
   - "unsolicited redesigns" that presume to fix a business without knowing its constraints,
   - spectacle aimed at the applicant's own audience.

   Founders and hiring managers say the novelty fades and only substance remains. [C: Ask a Manager; Aha! CEO Brian de Haaff; Jason Fried at 37signals; the Hacker News reaction to an unsolicited Zappos redesign]
4. **What makes the recipient act?** The successful cases share four features. [C, with B support]
   - **A specific, small ask.** Fedorin asked "Room for one more?"; KP applied to a posted role.
   - **A recipient who can say yes without a process.** The creative directors could hire; so could the founders at 37signals and On Deck.
   - **Readable in seconds.** Executives spend at most about nine seconds on an opened email ([Gong, 2026](https://www.gong.io/blog/do-execs-really-reply-to-cold-email-here-s-what-the-data-says)), and recruiters about 7.4 seconds on a CV ([HR Dive on Ladders, 2018](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/)).
   - **Visible value to the recipient.** When the response was public, it was also good publicity for the company.

   A scarce, costly signal of special interest raises acceptance, but only for candidates who already fit ([Lee & Niederle](https://web.stanford.edu/~niederle/Roses.Lee.Niederle.pdf)). [A, by analogy]
5. **What would change the planned approach?** Four things. *(Inference throughout, built on the evidence above.)*
   - **The six stakeholder sections are where the plan is most exposed to "creepy."** No case I found published a section on each of several named employees. The closest is Brownstein's five private ads. Sections framed as "what I'd do for your function", built from the person's own professional output with sources shown, sit on the safe side of every finding. Sections that read as a profile or dossier of the person sit on the unsafe side.
   - **The public build puts the recipients in a spotlight they didn't choose.** Public applications drew public replies when replying made the company look good. Nothing shows how named employees feel about being watched while they decide. A private route to respond or decline is the evidence-led hedge.
   - **AI involvement lowers perceived effort.** That matters because the effort signal is a big part of why the format works. Recipients who learn a message was AI-made rate the sender as less caring and less effortful (the "AI disclosure penalty", [Fast Company on Zhu & Molnar, 2026](https://www.fastcompany.com/91529584/ai-written-text-messages-research-study)). The build is public, so AI involvement is disclosed by design. The choice left is how to frame it.
   - **The message that delivers the link matters as much as the site.** Whatever carries the site to each stakeholder gets seconds, not minutes.

**Surveys of hiring managers [B]:**
- **CareerBuilder 2010** (2,778 hiring managers): 9% said they had hired someone who used an unconventional tactic, and 22% said they were seeing more such tactics (search excerpt of survey coverage, e.g. [CreativePro](https://creativepro.com/unusual-job-tactics/)).
- **CareerBuilder 2017** (over 2,000 hiring and HR managers): 26% said unusual attention-seeking antics would make them *less* likely to call a candidate in. The list of memorable stunts includes a candidate who "developed a whole website dedicated to the hiring manager, asking to be hired" ([HCA Magazine, 2017](https://www.hcamag.com/ca/archived/the-weirdest-applications-recruiters-get/155168)). That page's headline frames the list as ranging "from unusual to downright creepy". It doesn't say which stunts it means.
- **CareerBuilder 2015** coverage says the stunts get attention "but not necessarily the job" ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/hiring-managers-unveil-some-strange-stunts-pulled-by-job-applicants/403213)).

**Patterns:**
- **Attention is near-certain once the piece reaches the target, and it often comes from the top.** Every documented case drew a reply from a senior person. Getting it in front of them is the real filter: Tim's unviewed pitch, Fedorin's two failed videos. [C]
- **The hire depends on fit and on whether the recipient can act directly.**
  - The four hires at the target went to small companies (37signals, On Deck), to people who personally held hiring power (Brownstein's creative directors), or to a founder-led culture with a small, invented role (Shopify internship).
  - The three misses were large companies with formal processes (Google, Airbnb, Instagram).

  *(Inference from seven cases. Suggestive, not proven.)*
- **Senior people stress that the format is a tiebreaker, not a substitute.**
  - Fried: the extra mile scores "bonus points", mainly when finalists are close, and "We look for creativity, focus, and clarity" ([SvN](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/2709-sites-that-landed-jobs-at-37signals)).
  - De Haaff: "The stunt may get you noticed, but it unlikely to get you the job… After the novelty wears off, you need to show real substance" ([Aha!, 2016](https://www.aha.io/blog/the-wrong-way-to-make-your-resume-stand-out)). [C]
- **The public is a second market.** Epstein, Mufleh, Lee and KP all got interest from companies other than the target, because the work was public. [C]
- **Demos are relevant evidence, not just theatre.**
  - Work-sample tests are among the better predictors of job performance. Schmidt & Hunter (1998) put them at .54; Sackett et al. (2022) revised that to about .33, which is still in the upper group ([Cambridge chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/A20984B138319E3D432E643978BF026D/core-reader), [master-hr summary](https://master-hr.com/insights/new-study-providing-updated-validity-estimates/)). [A] I didn't read the primary text for the exact .33 figure; it is consistent with the chapter's "≈.21 lower for work sample tests".
  - So a site built from real GTM work for the company is closer to a work sample than to a cake with "hire me" on it. *(inference)*

### Q2. What tips it from impressive to creepy?

**The research [A]:**
- **Covert collection reverses the benefit of personalisation.**
  - Overt collection: more personalised ads raise click-through intentions. Covert collection: they lower them, because of the vulnerability people feel. A Facebook field study found sharp drops when people realised their data had been collected without consent.
  - Trust cues offset the effect.
  - Aguirre, Mahr, Grewal, de Ruyter & Wetzels, *Journal of Retailing* 91(1), 2015 ([City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/15747/)).
- **Unjustified fit triggers reactance.**
  - People push back when a highly personalised message matches their personal characteristics and the sender doesn't justify the match.
  - People who see high utility in the offer are less affected.
  - White, Zahay, Thorbjørnsen & Shavitt, *Marketing Letters* 19(1), 2008 ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs11002-007-9027-9)).
- **What matters is how the information travelled.**
  - Revealing "unacceptable" flows lowers ad effectiveness. Those are data taken from another site, or attributes the firm inferred rather than ones the person stated.
  - Revealing acceptable flows *raises* effectiveness when the platform is trusted.
  - Kim, Barasz & John, *Journal of Consumer Research* 45(5), 2019 ([BU summary](https://www.bu.edu/susilo/2020/05/30/can-online-ad-transparency-enhance-ad-effectiveness), [abstract](https://merit.url.edu/en/publications/why-am-i-seeing-this-ad-the-effect-of-ad-transparency-on-ad-effec-4/)).
- **Creepiness is ambiguity, and control and transparency reduce it.**
  - The Creepiness of Situation Scale (882 participants, US and Germany) finds two dimensions: emotional creepiness and creepy ambiguity.
  - Creepiness correlates *negatively* with controllability and transparency, and positively with privacy concern.
  - Langer & König, *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2018 ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02220/pdf)). Langer's follow-up work applies it to how job applicants react to selection technology.
- **Creepy means ambiguity about threat.**
  - Unpredictability and not knowing someone's intent drive the feeling of being creeped out.
  - The authors call the study exploratory.
  - McAndrew & Koehnke, *New Ideas in Psychology*, 2016 ([PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/collecting/2016-mcandrew.pdf)).
- **"Creepy" tracks shifting social norms and is subjective.**
  - That makes it hard to write a rule, so context is the guide.
  - Tene & Polonetsky, *Yale Journal of Law & Technology*, 2014 ([summary](https://thelivinglib.org/a-theory-of-creepy-technology-privacy-and-shifting-social-norms/)).

**Practitioner accounts [C]:**
- **Ask a Manager** (Alison Green, 2012): gimmicks "make you come across as overly aggressive, hokey, or even creepy", and they say nothing about your qualifications ([post](https://www.askamanager.org/2012/07/your-job-searching-gimmick-sucks-and-is-kind-of-creepy.html)). A later post in the same series adds that if one gets you hired, "you've just self-selected for a manager who responds to gimmicks over merit" ([tag page](https://www.askamanager.org/tag/gimmicks-wont-get-you-a-job)). Both come from search excerpts only; the pages were blocked.
- **Brownstein** said he wanted to "invade that secret, egotistical moment" when the directors searched themselves. Ben Welsh of M&C Saatchi said targeting creative directors when they Google themselves is great, but "Holding someone to ransom, not so much" ([AdNews](https://www.adnews.com.au/news/creative-focus-creatively-upping-the-ante); search excerpt). The targets' own reactions aren't on record.
- **Career-advice sources:**
  - Research the company, not the hiring manager's private life ([The Muse](https://themuse.com/advice/how-to-stalk-your-future-employer-without-being-creepy); search excerpt).
  - Anything a person "would not reasonably expect you to know from their public, professional presence can backfire" (outreach-vendor guidance, search excerpt).
  - A candidate contacted on a personal email address felt it signalled "you have been snooping around for my personal info" ([Blind thread](https://www.teamblind.com/post/recruiters-contacting-on-personal-email-kqnzke4b); search excerpt).
- **Commodity personalisation reads as fake.** "The page should prove I understand the prospect's problem, not just swap in a company name" ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/product/greve); search excerpt). Clay now ships a template to "auto-generate personalized landing pages for all prospects" ([Clay](https://www.clay.com/templates/auto-generate-personalized-landing-pages-for-all-prospects)). A personalised page alone is no longer a rare signal. *(inference)*

**Where the line sits.** A synthesis of the above, applied to a stakeholder section. *(inference, built on the A-grade findings)*

| Safer side | Creepier side | Finding behind it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Professional and role-level: their function, their public talks, posts and company announcements | Personal: family, hobbies, home, non-work social posts, personal photos | Context and norms (Kim et al.; Tene & Polonetsky; Muse) |
| Facts they published themselves, with the source shown | Facts that were inferred, scraped and combined, or taken from a context they didn't choose | Unacceptable flows (Kim et al.); covert collection (Aguirre et al.) |
| A stated reason for the fit ("you run X, so here is what I'd build for X") | Fit without justification ("I know this about you") | Reactance (White et al.) |
| A clear purpose, a clear ask and an obvious way to say no | Ambiguous intent, no exit, tracking they can't see | Ambiguity and control (Langer & König; McAndrew & Koehnke) |
| Something useful to them even if they say no | Something only useful to the applicant | High utility dampens reactance (White et al.) |

### Q3. What tips it from impressive to self-indulgent?

**Practitioner consensus [C]:**
- **Founders and managers:**
  - De Haaff calls stunts "sensational, desperate, self-serving, or juvenile" ([Aha!](https://www.aha.io/blog/the-wrong-way-to-make-your-resume-stand-out)).
  - Fried: "It's not about mimicking what's been done" ([SvN](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/2709-sites-that-landed-jobs-at-37signals)).
  - Green: gimmicks signal you don't trust your qualifications to stand alone (Ask a Manager, excerpts).
  - A 37signals reader: "There's a very fine line between clever and silly." Another was rebuked by a recruiter who read a custom PDF application as "a lack of respect" ([SvN thread](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/2713-recently-we-featured-creative-application-s)).
- **The "unsolicited redesign" genre is the closest analogue to building a company's work for it in public.**
  - Khoi Vinh, former design director at the New York Times, calls it "a public demonstration of talent" ([subtraction.com](https://subtraction.com/2011/07/28/unsolicited-redesigns); search excerpt, page not fetched).
  - When a designer posted a Zappos redesign to Hacker News, much of the criticism was aimed at his "presumptuousness" (search excerpt).
  - *(inference)* Work that claims to fix a company's business without knowing its constraints reads as being about the builder.
- **Performative stunts that don't match the role** backfire. The Fedorin write-up contrasts her tailored pitch with applicants who sent pizzas, skateboards and bricks: "They did not get the job." A commenter on that post called the story more "viral moment" than blueprint, adding "A paid internship isn't such a big price to pay", meaning Shopify got publicity in return ([Carly](https://carly.substack.com/p/how-to-get-a-job-in-24-hours)).
- **Public hiring stunts are judged harshly in 2026, even from the company side.** An SF AI startup's founder apologised ("I messed up") for offering job interviews in exchange for logo tattoos ([SFist, Jul 2026](https://sfist.com/2026/07/31/sf-ai-founder-says-he-messed-up-after-persuading-job-seekers-to-get-tattoo-of-company-logo/)). This is a different direction, but it shows how hiring theatre reads right now. [C]

**AI and the effort signal [A, B]:**
- **The disclosure penalty.**
  - Recipients rate a sender more harshly when told a personal message was AI-written. Without disclosure, they mostly can't tell (more than 1,300 US adults) ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91529584/ai-written-text-messages-research-study), [U-Michigan](https://prod.lsa.umich.edu/psych/news-events/all-news/faculty-news/most-people-do-not-realize-when-a-personal-message-they-receive-.html)).
  - Disclosure lowered ratings of trustworthiness, caring, competence and likability. Higher AI literacy softened the drop ([arXiv 2510.24011, IUI '26](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24011)).
- **Effort is part of the mechanism.**
  - People judge quality partly by the effort they think went in. The 2023 replication was mixed ([Effort heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effort_heuristic); Kruger et al. 2004, [U-Michigan](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/f5e6e3d7-57bd-483b-adcc-b10d2a62b8be/full)).
  - One boundary condition: people infer quality from effort only when an "effort" theory is active, not a "talent" theory (Cho & Schwarz 2008, via the same source).
  - *(inference)* A 12-hour AI-assisted build can't win on visible hours of effort. It can win on demonstrated capability, and capability is the job a GTM engineer is hired for.
- **B2B buyers in particular.**
  - A secondhand citation of Gartner's 2025 buyer study says 64% of buyers spot AI-generated outreach within the first sentence, and most say it lowers their willingness to engage ([Starr Conspiracy](https://www.thestarrconspiracy.com/insights/trends/brief-ai-lead-generation-outbound)). I didn't verify this against Gartner.
  - Firmable's survey of 1,009 professionals: 82% delete cold outreach on sight, and 61% distrust incoming AI outreach ([ContentGrip](https://www.contentgrip.com/firmable-ai-cold-outreach/)). [B, vendor]

**Building in public [C]:**
- KP named the applicant's own risk: "If I pulled a stunt like this and it fails, I would be laughed at in tech circles" ([KP](https://kp.substack.com/p/how-i-landed-my-dream-job-at-an-ambitious)).
- On Deck chose to make its offer "in public", so the recipient leaned into the public frame. The company was in the community-building business, and the public offer was on-brand for it. *(inference)*
- No source covers recipients who *didn't* want the spotlight. *(inference)* That is the gap: reactance theory predicts that pressure on someone's freedom to decide privately produces pushback. How much a public 12-hour build aimed at six named people creates that pressure is untested.

### Q4. What makes the recipient act?

- **A specific, small ask.**
  - Fedorin: "Room for one more?" The write-up's lessons are: lead with value, tailor the pitch to the company's culture, make a specific ask for a defined role, and show rather than tell ([Carly](https://carly.substack.com/p/how-to-get-a-job-in-24-hours)).
  - KP applied to a posted role, then told the recruiter honestly that it wasn't his best fit, and was offered a better one ([KP](https://kp.substack.com/p/how-i-landed-my-dream-job-at-an-ambitious)). [C]
- **Value first, a short message, a soft call to action.**
  - Gong (2026, analysis of "1M+ executive sales cycles"): C-level executives are 30.2% less likely to reply to cold email than others. Opened emails get about nine seconds.
  - Replies drop sharply above 100 words.
  - The call to action works best as "an offer of value" (a benchmark or insight) rather than a meeting request ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/do-execs-really-reply-to-cold-email-here-s-what-the-data-says)).
  - Gong Labs' older study of 304,174 emails favoured interest-based calls to action ("open to…?") for cold first touches. Secondary sources disagree on the exact rates ([Prospeo summary](https://prospeo.io/s/cold-email-call-to-action)). [B, vendor]
- **Readable within the first glance.** Ladders' eye-tracking puts the first screen of a CV at about 7.4 seconds. ERE notes the method isn't fully disclosed ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/), [ERE critique](https://www.ere.net/is-the-6-second-resume-scan-a-myth/)). [B] R1 covers making the work legible in depth.
- **A scarce, credible signal amplifies a fit; it doesn't create one.**
  - In a Korean online-dating field experiment (613 participants), attaching a scarce "rose" raised acceptance by about 3.3 percentage points, roughly 20%.
  - It did little for less desirable proposers ([Lee & Niederle, NBER/Experimental Economics](https://web.stanford.edu/~niederle/Roses.Lee.Niederle.pdf), [Slate](https://www.slate.com/articles/business/the_dismal_science/2012/02/internet_dating_how_digital_roses_can_make_it_a_better_experience_.html)).
  - The economics job market built a formal "signal of special interest", limited to two employers, on the same logic ([JEP 2010](https://stanford.edu/%7Ealroth/papers/AEA%20jobmarket%202010.pdf)). [A, by analogy]
  - *(inference)* A site built for one company is a strong signal *because* it is plainly not reusable. Generic sections weaken it.
- **Make yes easy for someone who can say it.**
  - Brownstein went after the people who could hire. The fastest yeses came from founders or executives who could create a role on the spot (Shopify, On Deck). [C]
  - *(inference)* Each stakeholder section should make clear what that person, in particular, could say yes to.
- **Give the company a reason to want to be seen responding.** In the public cases, the reply also made the company look good: Chesky's and Mildenhall's tweets, Shopify's "high agency" story, On Deck's public offer. [C] *(inference)* A public build that flatters the company's judgment invites a public yes. One that exposes or second-guesses individuals invites silence.

## Evidence gaps

- **No systematic outcome data.** I found no study of hire rates for sites aimed at one employer. Everything in Q1 is a named anecdote, skewed toward survivors. The "four of seven" figure describes the press record, not the odds.
- **No precedent for a section on each of several named employees on a public site.** The closest case is Brownstein's five private search ads. The DoD-18 format is untested in anything I could find. So is the combination of public, named and recorded live.
- **No GTM-engineering case.** Clay community and job-post searches showed that employers ask for portfolios and Looms. I found no documented case of a GTM engineer applying with company-specific pipeline work, or of how it was received. X and LinkedIn posts are poorly indexed by search, which is the likely reason.
- **No European or Czech evidence.** Every case is from the US, Canada, or a Jordanian applicant in the US. Norms on directness and privacy in a European B2B setting may differ. That is plausible, and I can't support it with evidence. R3 covers the legal side.
- **The creepiness research comes from advertising and consumer settings, not job applications.** Applying it to a job applicant addressing named employees is inference. Langer's work on applicant reactions is the nearest bridge, and it covers selection technology, not candidate outreach.
- **Targets' own words are scarce.** None of Brownstein's five directors is quoted on how it felt. Mufleh's final outcome comes only from secondary sources.
- **Some figures weren't checked against primary sources:**
  - the Gartner 64% (seen secondhand),
  - Gong Labs' CTA rates (sources conflict),
  - the CareerBuilder 9% (from survey coverage),
  - Sackett's .33 (derived).
- **Blocked pages.** For Ask a Manager, Quartz, subtraction.com and NPR, the quotes above come from search excerpts and should be re-checked before anyone quotes them publicly.

## Recommendations (evidence plus options; every call is Alex's)

1. **How the six stakeholder sections are framed (DoD-18).**
   - Option A, "what I'd do for your function": built from each person's role and their own published professional output, with sources shown. All the A-grade findings favour it.
   - Option B, a profile of the person: more striking, and it sits squarely on the creepy side of the table in Q2.
   - Option C: A, plus one line per section saying where every fact came from and that the person can ask for removal.

   *Evidence:* transparency and control cut creepiness (Langer & König); acceptable, stated flows raise effectiveness (Kim et al.). Option C also overlaps with R3's legal findings. Check them together.
2. **Public first or private first.**
   - Option A: send each stakeholder a private link and a short note before the public share, so they see it first and can reply or decline privately.
   - Option B: fully public, as recorded now.
   - Option C: public build, but stakeholder sections stay unlisted until the end.

   *Evidence:* the public cases worked when a public reply flattered the company. No evidence covers named individuals who didn't want the spotlight. Ambiguity and lack of control drive creepiness.
3. **The ask.** Decide what each section, and the site as a whole, asks for: a role, a call, or a reply. Make it one specific, small step.
   - *Evidence:* Fedorin's "Room for one more?"; Gong's value-first, interest-style call to action.
   - Option: make the ask an offer of value ("the pipeline and templates are yours to keep") rather than "hire me". Hand-over terms are Alex's call.
4. **Built for a first glance.** Each stakeholder section's point should be clear in under about ten seconds, and so should the message that carries the link. Keep it 50 to 100 words, with one link and one ask.
   - *Evidence:* Gong (about nine seconds, replies drop above 100 words); Ladders (about 7.4 seconds). R1 goes deeper.
5. **How AI is framed.** The build is public, so AI involvement is disclosed whatever happens.
   - Option A: name it up front as the capability on offer ("this is what one GTM engineer with agents ships in 12 hours").
   - Option B: foreground Alex's judgment calls: what he chose, cut and checked.
   - Option C: both.

   *Evidence:* the disclosure penalty works through perceived effort and care. The effort cue only helps when people are judging effort rather than talent (Cho & Schwarz). *(inference)* Showing judgment keeps "care" visible.
6. **Substance over spectacle.** Weigh every element of the site by what it shows the company, not how clever it looks.
   - Option: an editorial check before publishing. For each stakeholder section, ask: would this still be useful to them if Alex weren't applying? If not, it reads as self-indulgent.
   - *Evidence:* de Haaff, Fried, Green, and the unsolicited-redesign reception.
7. **Don't fix their business in public.** Frame the demos as "here's what I built with public information, and here's what I'd need from you to make it real", not as a verdict on how they run things.
   - *Evidence:* the "presumptuousness" reception of unsolicited redesigns. *(inference)*
8. **Plan for a no, and for the second market.** The documented upside is split between the target and everyone else who sees the work (Epstein, Mufleh, KP). Decide two things in advance:
   - what happens to the stakeholder sections if Addvery says no or asks for changes: remove, anonymise, or keep;
   - whether the site doubles as Alex's public portfolio.
9. **Measurement, carefully.** Knowing whether the site was seen matters: one 37signals reader's pitch drew no views. Per-person tracking links would count as tracking the recipient can't see, which sits on the creepy side of Q2. Options:
   - aggregate analytics only;
   - one plain link per stakeholder, with that disclosed;
   - no tracking.

   Check against R3.
