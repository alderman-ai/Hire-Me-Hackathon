# L-03 W3 Wildcard: will it be received?

**Question (verbatim):** When Addvery's leaders read a job applicant's blog post arguing that AI workflows are always a managed service and that every company must pay someone to reach its chosen level of AI skill, will they take it as insight or as a sales pitch for hiring him? And what in the writing decides which way it goes?

### Everything left open within the lens

1. **The conclusion pays the author.** Article 1 argues that companies can't buy an AI workflow once; they must keep paying someone to run it, or train, hire or bring in outside help. Alex is a GTM engineer looking for exactly that work. A reader who sees the conclusion coming may discount the whole argument.
2. **Applicant or vendor?** The slider's top rung is "external building", and Alex's note says companies "still ... need to compensate someone else". On a job-application site, a post that lands on "bring in someone external" can read as a freelancer's sales letter, not an employee's thinking.
3. **Telling senior readers what every company must do.** The thesis is prescriptive ("every company is going to have to decide"). Addvery's leaders didn't ask for it. Unrequested advice to powerful people has its own reception problem.
4. **Absolute claims in front of practitioners.** "There's no such thing as building an AI workflow as a product" is a strong absolute. Readers who sell productised services may stop at the headline. (R6 covers whether it's true; this is whether the absolute costs trust.)
5. **The quality bar for thought leadership.** B2B readers rate most of it mediocre. A post that restates a common industry view earns nothing.
6. **Will the six read 900 to 1,400 words at all?** Mostly R4 (length) and L-01 W2 (what reaches them).
7. **An AI-drafted post arguing AI needs humans.** Coherent or ironic? Mostly L-01 W3, and the "How this post was made" box already answers it.
8. **The wider public.** Freelance prospects reading the same post. L-01 W3 passed this over too.

### Why this question, and what I passed over

I picked rank 1 because it's the only open question specific to article 1's *argument*, and the argument is what Alex asked for. Every other item asks how to make the post well or whether its claims hold. None asks how the claims sound from this author to this audience. Three things make it urgent:
- The article is self-serving by construction. Its conclusion is that companies must pay someone like Alex.
- It sits on a site whose whole purpose is "hire me", so the reader can't miss the interest.
- The dictation hasn't happened. The talking prompts can still change what Alex says.

I folded in rank 2 (absolutes) as a sub-point, since the same research covers it, and used rank 3's survey data as the bar the post has to clear. Passed over: rank 4 (R4 and L-01 W2 hold it), rank 5 (L-01 W3 settles it), rank 6 (anecdote only).

## Part (b) Research

### Method

About 15 minutes, web only. Searches and page reads across four bodies of evidence:
1. Persuasion research: the persuasion knowledge model, communicator self-interest, and message sidedness (one-sided versus two-sided arguments).
2. Advice research: unrequested and imposed advice, and power.
3. Self-promotion research.
4. B2B thought-leadership surveys (the Edelman and LinkedIn series, 2017 to 2025) and employer screening surveys (CareerBuilder).

Also a read-only look at `blog-pipeline\prompts\` to match the recommendations to what exists. Nothing about Addvery was researched (Alex's own, DI-3 Q3.3).

Limits: Edelman's own pages and PDFs returned HTTP 403, so its figures come from Edelman's own write-ups as quoted in search results and from trade press. The classic persuasion papers were read at abstract, textbook or press-release level. Everything is marked as such below. No study of this exact case exists: a job applicant's thought leadership, read by the employer he's targeting.

### Verdict up front

1. **The pitch will be seen whatever Alex does.** On a "hire me" site the reader knows the writer's goal from the first line. The question isn't whether to hide the interest but how the post handles it.
2. **A one-sided argument that ends where the author gets paid is discounted.** Readers credit a claim less when the speaker's interest explains it.
3. **Seen persuasion can raise credibility when the tactic is credible.** The tactics with the best evidence are a fair statement of the strongest objection followed by an answer to it, and a concession against the writer's own interest. For article 1 that means saying plainly when a company *doesn't* need anyone like Alex.
4. **"Every company must" lands worse on senior readers who didn't ask.** In one experiment, high-power people were the least willing to use advice imposed on them. Framing the slider as a tool the reader uses to place themselves keeps a clear view without imposing a verdict.
5. **Alex is the worst judge of how strong the pitch reads.** People who promote themselves overestimate how well it lands and underestimate the annoyance, so the post needs a cold read.
6. **The post is a work sample before it's an argument.** B2B decision-makers treat thought leadership as evidence of capability, rate most of it mediocre, and lose respect after weak pieces. The post's quality is the signal; the pitch is incidental.

**In three lines:** Addvery's leaders will see the pitch either way. It costs credibility only if the post argues in one direction, towards paying Alex. A post that names the strongest objection, concedes where outside help isn't needed, lets readers place themselves on the slider and keeps the ask to one line at the end reads as insight.

### Evidence

**E1. The intent is visible, so readers' persuasion knowledge is switched on.**
- Friestad and Wright's persuasion knowledge model (Journal of Consumer Research, 1994) holds that people carry working knowledge of persuaders' goals and tactics and use it to "cope" with a persuasion attempt, which the model defines from the target's side: the target's perception of an agent's strategic behaviour aimed at influencing them ([RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v21y1994i1p1-31.html)). Read at abstract level.
- Inference: the site's purpose and the post's thesis together make the attempt obvious. Each of the six reads article 1 knowing its author wants a job.

**E2. A position the speaker's interest explains is discounted. A position against it persuades more.**
- A standard social psychology text, citing Eagly, Wood and Chaiken (1978) and Wood and Eagly (1981), says people seen to argue in their own self-interest "may be ineffective because we may discount their communications". A message that runs against what the speaker's situation predicts leads readers to infer real belief ([Principles of Social Psychology, ch. 4](https://opentextbc.ca/socialpsychology/chapter/changing-attitudes-through-persuasion/)). Eagly, Wood and Chaiken 1978: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 36, 424–435 (citation confirmed in [Wendy Wood's CV](https://msbfile03.usc.edu/digitalmeasures/wlwood/pci/vita%20wendy%20wood-1.pdf)). Textbook level; abstract not fetched.
- Walster, Aronson and Abrahams (1966), "On increasing the persuasiveness of a low prestige communicator" (Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 2, 325–342) is the classic study usually cited for this. A low-prestige speaker arguing against his own interest persuaded as well as a high-prestige one. The citation is confirmed; that summary of the finding is from my own knowledge of the literature, not fetched. **Treat it as unverified.**
- Inference: "always a managed service, so you'll have to pay someone" is the in-interest position for a GTM engineer. "Here is when you should train your own people and pay nobody" is the against-interest one.

**E3. Recognising persuasion can raise credibility, when the tactic is credible.**
- Isaac and Grayson (Journal of Consumer Research 43(6), 2017), four studies: accessing persuasion knowledge "can lead to greater credibility (rather than greater skepticism)", and the effect is larger when the agent uses a credible tactic. Consumers then "evaluate the agent and its offering more (rather than less) favorably" ([Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2017/beyond-skepticism-can-accessing-persuasion-knowledge-bolster-credibility/)). The summary doesn't say which tactics they tested.
- Inference: the visible pitch (E1) isn't fatal in itself. What the reader sees Alex *doing* with it decides.

**E4. Two-sided arguments that answer the other side beat one-sided ones. Two-sided arguments that leave it unanswered do worst.**
- O'Keefe's 1999 meta-analysis pooled 107 studies with 20,111 participants. Two-sided messages that refute the opposing view were the most persuasive. Two-sided messages that only mention it were *less* persuasive than one-sided ones. It found no support for one-sided messages working better on friendly or less educated audiences ([summary at PsyBlog](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=12521); secondary).
- Allen and colleagues found the same order across 17 messages ([ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/testing-a-model-of-message-sidedness-three-replications)).
- In advertising, two-sided messages tend to be rated more credible because they admit weaknesses (Eisend 2006, International Journal of Research in Marketing 23, 187–198, as cited by a [2022 study](https://edisciplinas.usp.br/pluginfile.php/7947675/mod_resource/content/1/2022%20Int%20J%20Consumer%20Studies%20When%20transparency%20pays%20off%20Enticing%20sceptical%20consumers%20with%20two%E2%80%90sided.pdf)). Secondary; effect size not seen.
- Caution: O'Keefe and Hoeken's 2021 review of 1,149 studies finds message-design effects are generally small and not consistent from one message to the next ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.664160/pdf)). The two-sided advantage is real on average, not a guarantee.
- Inference: merely mentioning "some say AI workflows can be products" without answering it is the worst of the three options. R6 supplies the strongest counterarguments; the post has to answer them, not just list them.

**E5. Advice nobody asked for, aimed at powerful readers.**
- Van Swol, Prahl, MacGeorge and Branch (Communication Reports 32(3), 2019): imposed advice, given after the recipient said they didn't want it, produced "lower utilization intentions and positive emotions" than permitted or guaranteed advice. "High power participants had lower intentions than low power participants to use imposed advice" ([Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/imposing-advice-on-powerful-people/)).
- A study on advice solicitation found unsolicited advice was rated less useful and used less, but only when the recipient wasn't open to advice, mediated by face threat ([Taylor & Francis figshare](https://tandf.figshare.com/articles/The_contextual_effects_of_advice_solicitation_on_advice_outcomes_The_role_of_perceived_face_threats_and_psychological_reactance/11835672); authors not shown in the record).
- Counterweight: a 2024 Psychological Science paper reportedly found that advisers who leave the decision to the advisee, rather than prescribing, are judged *less* competent and less helpful ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976241268630); page returned 403; known only from a search summary, unverified).
- Inference: a blog post is broadcast, not personal advice, so this transfers only partly. On a site addressed to six named leaders, though, "every company is going to have to decide" reads as addressed to them. Taken together, the advice studies point to a clear view framed as the reader's own decision, not vagueness.

**E6. Self-promoters misjudge how it lands.**
- Scopelliti, Loewenstein and Vosgerau (Psychological Science 26(6), 2015): self-promoters overestimate the positive feelings and underestimate the annoyance their self-promotion causes. In experiment 3, recipients rated them less likeable and as braggarts ([City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6165/); [CMU release](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2015/may/bragging-researchers-find-self-promotion-often-backfires.html)).
- Inference: Alex's own sense of whether article 1 "sells too hard" is biased towards "it's fine". So is a drafting agent briefed to make his case.

**E7. The bar: B2B readers judge capability by thought leadership, and most of it disappoints them.**
- 2024 Edelman-LinkedIn report (3,484 business leaders, surveyed December 2023): 73% of B2B decision-makers say an organisation's thought leadership is "a more trustworthy basis for assessing its capabilities and competencies than its marketing materials and product sheets" (Edelman's own write-up, quoted in search; the [report PDF](https://www.edelman.com/sites/g/files/aatuss191/files/2024-02/_2024%20Edelman-LinkedIn%20B2B%20Thought%20Leadership%20Impact%20Report%20Final.pdf) returned 403). On quality, 30% call what they read mediocre and only 13% very good or excellent (search summary, secondary; see also [Curzon PR's summary](https://curzonpr.com/theprinsider/the-impact-of-thought-leadership-edelman-and-linkedin-b2b-2024-report/)).
- 2017 edition (1,300+ decision-makers and C-suite): weak thought leadership at times lost an organisation respect for 45% of decision-makers and 53% of C-level executives ([LinkedIn Marketing Solutions blog, 2017](https://business.linkedin.com/marketing-solutions/blog/marketing-for-tech-companies/2017/is-thought-leadership-earning-or-losing-business-decision-makers)).
- 2025 edition: 53% of decision-makers say that when a company's thought leadership is strong, brand recognition matters less ([Demand Gen Report](https://www.demandgenreport.com/industry-news/news-brief/marketers-need-to-gear-content-to-hidden-b2b-buyers-edelman-linkedin/49901/)). Inference: strong writing can make up for being unknown, which is Alex's position with the six.
- Context for 2026: LinkedIn added a "Seems like AI slop" report button this summer ([AlternativeTo, July 2026](https://alternativeto.net/news/2026/7/linkedin-adds-a-seems-like-ai-slop-button-to-report-low-quality-ai-generated-content/)). Readers are primed to spot generic AI thought leadership (L-01 W3 holds the evidence on the penalty).
- These surveys measure companies' content read by buyers, not an applicant's post read by an employer. Inference: the transfer is that the post is judged as a sample of how Alex thinks.

**E8. What employers credit when they look at a candidate's public content.**
- CareerBuilder/Harris Poll 2017: 70% of employers screen candidates' social media, and 44% had found content that led them to hire someone ([Staffing Industry Analysts](https://www.staffingindustry.com/news/global-daily-news/social-media-candidate-screening-record-high-survey-finds)). Among reasons that made employers more likely to hire: information supporting the candidate's qualifications (37%) and creativity (34%) ([Recruiter.com](https://www.recruiter.com/i/careerbuilder-social-media-posts-more-frequently-affect-hiring-decisions/); survey year not stated).
- Old, US, about social media rather than blogs. Inference only: public content helps a candidate when it proves the skill, which a self-serving thesis alone doesn't do.

### Bearing on the plan (inference throughout)

- **The talking prompts** (`prompts\0-talking-prompts.md`, from S-D4's methodology) ask for the problem, a story, what people get wrong, what he does instead and what to do on Monday. None asks for the best argument against him or the case where nobody should be hired. Those are what E2 to E4 favour, and they have to come from Alex's mouth. The edit stage can't invent them under the pipeline's "no claim that isn't in the dictation" rule.
- **The edit prompt** already says that where a post touches a real company's market, "it offers ideas, never a verdict on how that company runs its business" (`prompts\5-edit.md`). E5 supports that rule. It doesn't yet cover "every company must" aimed at the reader.
- **The slider** is the post's strongest asset for reception. It's already a self-placement tool if it's written as one: readers place themselves, and the bottom rung (train your own people) is the against-interest concession.
- **Applicant or vendor.** Alex's scope ends on "they're going to have to ... bring in someone external or hire someone external". For an employer, the rung that matches a job application is "hire". A conclusion that lands on "external building" reads like a vendor pitch (E1 to E2). If Addvery sells to clients who face this choice, the same argument could read as something Addvery could offer its own clients, with Alex as the person to build it. Whether that fits Addvery is Alex's call from his own research; nothing here assumes it.

### Evidence gaps

- No study of the exact case: a job applicant's thought-leadership post read by the employer he's targeting. Everything above transfers from persuasion, advice and B2B buyer research, and the transfer is inference.
- Edelman's primary pages returned 403. Its figures come from its own write-ups quoted in search results, a 2017 LinkedIn blog and trade press. The 2025 report was seen only through one trade article.
- The classic papers (Walster 1966, Eagly 1978, O'Keefe 1999, Eisend 2006) were read at citation, textbook or secondary-summary level. Walster's finding is from memory and unverified.
- The 2024 Psychological Science counterweight in E5 is unverified (403).
- Nothing on agency leaders specifically, or on European or Czech readers.
- Whether the six read the post at all is outside this report (L-01 W2: most will only see the short note).
- Nothing about Addvery was researched, by rule. How its leaders actually think about managed services is Alex's own knowledge.

## Part (c) Recommendations

Evidence plus options. Every call is Alex's. Tags: **assistant-actionable** (S-D4 or S-D5 can do it without him), **operator decision** (his call), **verify first**.

**C1. Add two talking prompts for article 1** (operator decision; S-D5 writes them into the dictation task). Evidence: E2, E4, and the "no claim that isn't in the dictation" rule.
- A. Add "What's the strongest argument against you, and what's your answer?" and "When should a company train its own people and hire nobody, including you?" Both answers come from Alex.
- B. Add only the first.
- C. Keep the five prompts and let R6's counterarguments go in as `[Alex: ...]` markers for his last 10%. Cheaper on talking time, but the answers arrive as edits, not in his voice.

**C2. A check at the edit stage: the strongest objection is stated fairly and answered** (assistant-actionable, a PIPELINE FIX-style change S-D4 owns). Evidence: E4. Answered, not just mentioned: mentioning without answering scored worst. If the dictation holds no objection, the editor leaves an `[Alex: the best argument against this, and your answer]` marker rather than writing one.

**C3. Write the slider as a self-placement tool, not a verdict** (assistant-actionable at outline and format; the rungs are Alex's). Evidence: E5, and R4's case for a diagram.
- Three or four rungs from his scope (use others' workflows, maintain them, build them, bring in outside builders), each with one line on who it fits and what it costs.
- The post asks "where are you?" rather than "you must".
- Keep a clear view of which rung suits which company, since vagueness may read as less competent (E5, unverified).

**C4. Choose which rung the conclusion lands on** (operator decision). Evidence: E1, E2, and the applicant-or-vendor inference above.
- A. Land on "someone inside who can maintain and extend it", which matches a job application.
- B. Stay neutral across rungs and let the reader choose. Most credible, weakest as a pitch.
- C. Land on "external building", as in his scope note. It fits the freelance route, but on this site it reads as a vendor pitch.

**C5. Keep the ask out of the argument** (operator decision on wording; it's the `[Alex: closing ask]` marker already in the pipeline). Evidence: E1, E3, E6.
- A. One plain line at the end, separate from the argument, naming the interest: for example, "This is the work I want to do, and it's why I'm applying to Addvery."
- B. No ask in the post; the site's own ask carries it.
- C. Name the interest in the opening instead ("I build these for a living, so weigh this accordingly") and end without an ask.

**C6. Keep the headline's absolute, but concede in the body** (operator decision on the title). Evidence: E4. "No such thing as an AI workflow product" can stay as the provocation if the body names where something close to a product does work (R6 lists the cases) and says why it still needs looking after. An absolute with no concession invites the practitioner reader to stop at the first counterexample.

**C7. A cold read before Alex's last 10%** (assistant-actionable). Evidence: E6. A fresh subagent, given only the draft and the brief "you lead a B2B agency and this is from someone applying to work for you", marks every sentence that reads as selling and every "you must". The edit stage already uses a fresh subagent, so this could be one more pass or a second brief. Alex reads the marks with the draft, then decides.

**C8. Hold the quality bar over the pitch** (assistant-actionable at the edit stage). Evidence: E7, E8.
- The post is judged as a sample of his thinking, and most thought leadership reads as mediocre to the people it targets.
- One specific story or number from his own work outweighs a paragraph of argument. The talking prompts' "a story where he saw it" already asks for one.
- If the dictation has none, it's the first `[Alex: ...]` marker, not something the draft makes up.

**Verify first.** Before quoting any Edelman figure on the site or in the post, check it against the original report: three figures here are secondary. Walster 1966 and the 2024 advice paper aren't cited anywhere public until read in full.

**One-line questions for Alex** (each names the spec it affects):
1. Add the two talking prompts (objection, and "when to hire nobody") to article 1's dictation task? (S-D5, C1)
2. Which rung should article 1's conclusion land on: inside hire, neutral, or external? (S-D5, C4)
3. Where does the ask go: one line at the end, none, or naming the interest up top? (S-D5, C5)
