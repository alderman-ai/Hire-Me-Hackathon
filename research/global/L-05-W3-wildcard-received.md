### Verdict up front

1. **The company-voice demo and the templates are on the gift side, if framed as tools handed to the team.** The risks there are territorial (the people who make this work today) and an implied critique, and framing can steer both. Brand teams already worry that AI gets their voice wrong, so a demo that *measures* voice fidelity speaks to a known worry ([Kelleher et al. 2019](https://www.jou.ufl.edu/insights/employee-perceptions-of-ceo-ghost-posting/) for voice; [eMarketer on HubSpot](https://www.emarketer.com/content/bank-marketers-nervous-ai-reputation-brand) for the worry).
2. **Posts in a named leader's own voice, unasked and public, sit on the overreach side on every strand of evidence found.** Ghostwriting is accepted as a consented, disclosed relationship, and this would be neither. Imitating a person is read as a threat to the self. The person can't approve it. Unsolicited advice is discounted as self-serving. Czech law protects a person's "projevy osobní povahy" (expressions of a personal nature). S-D7's current rule is consistent with all of this.
3. **A personal-branding *strategy* without posts in their voice sits in between.** It's unsolicited advice about someone's self-presentation. That gets discounted unless the person is receptive, and nothing tested in the main workplace study removed the "self-serving" reading ([Landis, Fisher & Menges](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10117618/1/How%20Employees%20React%20to%20Unsolicited%20Advice%20-%20UCL%20Discovery.pdf)).
4. **Receptiveness and perceived intent are the levers.** The face-threat penalty on unsolicited advice appeared only when receptiveness was absent. In territoriality research, perceived intent moderates how hard people defend. So the leader direction lands best as an *offer* the leader can take up in private, not as a published artefact (inference).
5. **No study tests this exact case.** The verdict combines four adjacent bodies of evidence, and points 2 to 4 are partly inference.

### Evidence

#### 1. Ghostwriting is accepted as a consented, disclosed relationship

- **Kelleher, Men & Thelen, *Public Relations Journal* 12(4), 2019**, on employees' views of CEO "ghost posting" ([UF summary](https://www.jou.ufl.edu/insights/employee-perceptions-of-ceo-ghost-posting/); [IPR](https://instituteforpr.org/how-ceo-ghost-posting-affects-employee-perceptions-on-leadership-transparency-and-relationships); a search summary gives 549 US employees):
  - Ghost posting was "deemed acceptable when accompanied by a clear disclosure"; employees were less tolerant when it wasn't disclosed.
  - "Employees who knew their CEO used ghost posting perceived their leadership authenticity in a less favorable light."
  - "A conversational CEO voice was positively perceived in terms of authentic leadership", whether or not the CEO had help.
- **Practitioner norms.** Executive ghostwriting guides describe the same workflow: an interview recorded with consent, a draft, the executive's review, approval recorded against the final version, and material edits going back for re-approval ([Vulse](https://vulse.co/blog/linkedin-ghostwriting-for-executives); [Writio](https://writio.ai/blog/ghostwrite-linkedin-posts-executives-without-losing-voice); [MagicPost](https://magicpost.in/blog/linkedin-ghostwriting-services): name the client only with consent). These are vendor blogs, not a standard, but they agree.
- *Inference:* even legitimate ghostwriting costs a leader some perceived authenticity, and it's tolerated because the leader chose it, approved it and disclosed it. Unasked posts in a named leader's voice would carry the authenticity cost with none of those three conditions, and the leader would learn about it from a public page.

#### 2. Imitating a person reads as a threat to the self

- **Lee, Ma, Kim & Yoon, "Speculating on Risks of AI Clones to Selfhood and Relationships", *Proc. ACM HCI* 7 (CSCW1), 2023** ([doi 10.1145/3579524](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F3579524); [The Conversation summary](https://theconversation.com/ai-clones-made-from-user-data-pose-uncanny-risks-206357)). Twenty participants reacted to eight speculative AI-clone scenarios. Three risks came up:
  - *doppelgänger-phobia*: fear of the copy being misused to take over or replace one's identity;
  - *identity fragmentation*: copying erodes what makes a person distinct;
  - *misrepresentation*: a copy speaking for someone others know.
  The sample is small and the scenarios hypothetical.
- **L-01 W3 (parent, not repeated):** outsourcing relational writing to AI drew the harshest judgement, and that held through honest disclosure and good writing (Kent, 2026).
- *Inference:* a voice rubric of a named leader, used to write posts they never said, is a small text-only version of that clone. The company voice doesn't trigger this, because a brand voice is a shared, deliberately designed asset rather than a self.

#### 3. Unsolicited advice is discounted, and read as self-serving

- **Landis, Fisher & Menges, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2021 online, 2022 in print, pp. 408–424.** They used three studies: an organisational network study, a two-week experience-sampling study and an experiment ([UCL preprint](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10117618/1/How%20Employees%20React%20to%20Unsolicited%20Advice%20-%20UCL%20Discovery.pdf); [SPSP summary](https://spsp.org/news-center/character-context-blog/reactions-unsolicited-advice-workplace); [Cambridge Judge](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/2021/tainted-suggestions/)):
  - Recipients attribute self-serving motives to unsolicited advice and prosocial motives to solicited advice.
  - No variable they tested significantly reduced that negative attribution (search summary of the paper).
  - The authors' practical tip, via SPSP, is to frame advice so self-interest is less easily inferred (secondary, not a headline result).
- **"The contextual effects of advice solicitation on advice outcomes: the role of perceived face threats and psychological reactance"** ([Taylor & Francis figshare](https://tandf.figshare.com/articles/The_contextual_effects_of_advice_solicitation_on_advice_outcomes_The_role_of_perceived_face_threats_and_psychological_reactance/11835672); abstract via search only, page 403):
  - Unsolicited advice was rated lower in utility and was less likely to be acted on, **"but only when the receptiveness to advice was absent"**.
  - Negative face threat mediated the effect.
- *Inference:* a public personal-branding strategy for a named leader is advice about how that person should present themself, which is more face-threatening than advice about a company asset. And in a job application the self-serving motive is real and visible. Receptiveness is the one lever the research shows working, and a private offer creates it in a way a public page can't.

#### 4. The marketing team: territory, intent, and the insider who defends the work

- **Brown, Lawrence & Robinson, "Territoriality in Organizations", *Academy of Management Review* 30(3), 2005**: people form territories over spaces, ideas, roles and relationships, and they mark, defend and restore them ([SMU](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/2435)).
- **Brown & Robinson, "Reactions to Territorial Infringement", *Organization Science* 22(1), 2011** ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v22y2011i1p210-224.html); [SMU](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/1755)). In a survey of 125 employees' critical incidents:
  - perceived infringement triggers anger, which drives defensive reactions;
  - a working-paper abstract, quoted in a search summary, says reaction strength rose with psychological ownership and varied by type of infringement, was mediated by negative emotion, and **was moderated by perceived intent**.
- **Brown, Crossley & Robinson, *Personnel Psychology*, 2014:** a high-trust environment reduces the territorial behaviour that ownership produces (abstract via search).
- **The case: Dustin Curtis and American Airlines, 2009.** A designer published an unsolicited redesign of AA.com as an open letter. An AA insider replied privately, explaining that good design was hard inside a large company and that better work was coming. AA fired him for it ([Gadling](https://gadling.com/?p=22113); [D Magazine](https://www.dmagazine.com/frontburner/2009/05/american-airlines-website-gets-a-hostile-makeover/); accounts differ on his role). Gadling notes AA may have read the post as a pitch for Curtis's services.
- **Creative identity threat.** Fifty-six professional designers described generative AI as challenging their expertise and diluting their distinctiveness ([Dowsett, Maiden & van Werven, EGOS 2024](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/33503/)). No study I found covered marketers or content writers specifically.
- *Inference:* this territoriality research is about coworkers, not outsiders. Still, the reader most likely to defend is whoever makes Addvery's LinkedIn posts and visuals today, and that may be someone the CMO trusts. A public remake reads to them as "here is your job, done by an outsider and an AI". Whether it reads as an attack or as help depends on perceived intent, which framing can show.

#### 5. What can tip it to the gift side

- **The worry it answers.** Marketers fear AI misrepresents their brand:
  - 60% of 1,400 marketers were wary of brand repercussions from AI-written content, including misalignment (HubSpot, Sept 2023, via [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/bank-marketers-nervous-ai-reputation-brand));
  - 16% say AI output doesn't match their brand's tone of voice (GfK, via [Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/ai-creativity-b2b-linkedin-consumer-confidence-5-interesting-stats/));
  - 40% of Australian marketing leaders name AI misrepresenting the brand as their biggest concern ([Marketing-Interactive, 2026](https://www.marketing-interactive.com/australian-marketers-fear-ai-is-getting-their-brands-wrong)).
  *Inference:* S-D7's scored posts and blind judge prove control over the voice, which is the thing these marketers say they lack. That reads as solving their problem, not replacing their people.
- **Tools, not replacements.** Templates "anyone can fill in" with brand elements locked (S-D6) hand control *to* the team. *Inference:* framed as "for your team to use", they extend the team's territory instead of taking it.
- **Gift-first outreach.** Practitioner and vendor sources claim that offering something made for the prospect lifts replies ([Fuzzy](https://getfuzzy.ai/blog/reference-prospect-linkedin-post-cold-email)). These are self-reported vendor figures; I found nothing measuring whether a "rewrote your post" opener reads as flattering or intrusive.

#### 6. The legal edge for a named person's voice (Czech law, not legal advice)

- **Civil Code (89/2012 Sb.) § 81(2)** protects "jeho vážnost, čest, soukromí a jeho projevy osobní povahy": a person's standing, honour, privacy and expressions of a personal nature. **§§ 84–85:** capturing or spreading a person's likeness needs their consent ([podnikatel.cz, in force from 1 Jan 2026](https://www.podnikatel.cz/zakony/novy-obcansky-zakonik/f4579101/); [Carter-Ruck guide](https://www.carter-ruck.com/law-guides/defamation-and-privacy-law-in-czech-republic)).
- *Inference:* text isn't likeness, but posts presented as a named person's own words, published without their consent, sit close to the protected "expressions of a personal nature" and "standing". A company voice carries no such personal right. I found no Czech case law on ghostwritten or AI-written text attributed to a real person.
- L-01 R3 covers GDPR and LinkedIn's terms; nothing here is posted to LinkedIn.

### Evidence gaps

- **No study of unasked ghostwriting** or of being shown posts written in one's own voice without asking. Points 2 and 3 of the verdict combine adjacent work.
- **The ghost-posting study samples employees judging their own CEO**, not a leader judging an outsider. The clone study has 20 people and hypothetical scenarios.
- **Territoriality research covers coworkers.** I found nothing on outsiders remaking a team's work for a job application.
- **Several findings rest on abstracts or search summaries:** Brown & Robinson's moderators, Brown, Crossley & Robinson, and the advice-solicitation study (page 403).
- **Marketer surveys are 2023 or vendor-reported.** The GfK figure's date wasn't confirmed.
- **Nothing on Czech or Central European business norms** around ghostwriting or unsolicited advice. All studies are US, UK or Western European. *Inference:* norms of personal directness may differ, which cuts both ways.
- **No legal check:** no Czech case law on attributed text, and not legal advice.
- **The gift-first outreach claims are vendor figures**, unverified.

## Part (c) Recommendations

These are evidence and options. Every call is Alex's. Tags: **operator decision**, **assistant-actionable** (a spec can do it once he says so), **verify first**.

1. **The leader direction (S-D7; operator decision).** Alex's note made this lower priority. Options, from least to most exposed:
   - **A. Keep S-D7's rule:** company voice only, and drop the leader direction for today. *Evidence:* sections 1 to 3 and 6. It costs nothing and keeps the current plan.
   - **B. Method only, no named person:** a short, generic "how I'd run a company leader's LinkedIn with them" panel on the site (the method from R4: interview, voice capture, approval loop), with sample posts in a clearly fictional leader's voice. It shows the skill without putting words in anyone's mouth. *Evidence:* sections 1 and 2 (consent and approval are the method, so showing them is the pitch).
   - **C. A private offer:** prepare the leader strategy, but don't publish it. One line on the site or in the message offers it ("If it's useful, I've sketched how I'd build a leader's LinkedIn presence with them; happy to walk through it"). The leader opts in. *Evidence:* section 3 (receptiveness is the lever) and L-01 R4's one small ask.
   - **D. Named leader, strategy only, public:** a plan with no posts in their voice. *Evidence:* section 3 says it gets discounted as self-serving, and L-01 R4's creepy line applies to a named person.
   - **E. Named leader, posts in their voice, public.** Every strand in section 7 points against it.
   B and C can be combined. If he picks D or E, it's worth the brand sign-off asking whether he has any signal that the leader would welcome it, since receptiveness is what the evidence turns on.

2. **The baseline frame (S-D7; assistant-actionable).** Keep the contrast as "generic AI on the same topic" against "AI held to a voice rubric", never against Addvery's own published posts. S-D7 already keeps their posts off the site. One sentence can name the problem it solves: AI that sounds like everyone else. *Evidence:* section 5 (the worry) and section 4 (implied critique raises territorial defence).

3. **Hand-over framing for the templates (S-D6, S-D10; assistant-actionable).** In the D6 section, say who they're for and who owns them: "built for your team to fill in; locked where the brand is, editable where the message is; yours to keep or bin." Avoid "fixing" or "before and after" language. S-D10's video of Alex filling a template already shows the tool in a person's hands, which supports this. *Evidence:* section 4 (intent moderates) and section 5 (tools extend territory). *Inference.*

4. **Say the intent once, plainly (S-D8; operator decision on wording).** One line where the D6 and D7 sections start: what this is (an unofficial demo for a job application, not affiliated with Addvery), and that it's made for their team to judge and use. That's L-01 W3's label plus an intent line. *Evidence:* section 3 (the self-serving reading is strongest when the motive is hidden; here it's stated) and section 4 (perceived intent). *Inference.*

5. **One reception check at the 16:45 brand sign-off (S-D6, S-D7; operator decision).** Add one plain question to both gate tasks: "If the person who makes Addvery's LinkedIn posts and visuals today saw this, would they feel helped or replaced?" Fix any line that fails it. *Evidence:* section 4. It costs Alex about a minute.

6. **If any personal voice is ever used (verify first).** It would need the person's consent and approval before publishing, the norm in section 1. And under section 6, ask a Czech lawyer before any named person's attributed words go public.
