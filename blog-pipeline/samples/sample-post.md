For years my week ended the same way. Friday evening came, and I had a vague feeling that something important had slipped, with no idea what it was.

A weekly review fixed that. Not the two-hour ritual some people describe, but fifteen minutes with a notebook, a calendar and a short list of questions. This is the version I actually keep doing, and how to set up your own.

> [!summary]
> - A weekly review only survives if it is short enough to do on a bad week.
> - Keep the questions on one card, so you never decide what to look at.
> - Spend the time looking back, then looking ahead, then choosing three things.
> - Leave out anything that turns the review into a planning session.

## Why most weekly reviews don't last

I have started a weekly review at least four times. Each time I built something thorough: every project, every list and every inbox, checked and sorted. It felt great for about three weeks.

Then a busy Friday came and I skipped it. A skipped review is twice as long the following week, so the next one felt even heavier. My longest early attempt lasted [Alex: how many weeks your longest early attempt lasted] before it quietly stopped.

The problem was never discipline. The problem was size. A review that needs an hour loses to almost anything else you could do with that hour. And the weeks you need it most are the weeks you have the least time.

So the rule I work by now is simple. If I can't do it in fifteen minutes on a tired Friday, it doesn't belong in the review.

> [!pull]
> A review that needs an hour loses to almost anything else you could do with that hour.

## Set it up once

The setup takes about ten minutes, and you only do it once. You need three things, and you probably own all of them already.

- **A fixed slot in your calendar.** Mine is Friday at four, when the week is mostly done but there is still time to fix something.
- **One landing place for loose tasks.** A notebook, a notes app, or a single page that everything goes onto during the week.
- **A card with the review questions on it.** Paper or a pinned note, as long as it is the same card every week.

The slot matters more than the tools. I treat it like a meeting with someone I don't want to let down, and I move it rather than delete it when the week gets in the way. The idea is close to [timeboxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeboxing): the fifteen minutes are fixed, and the review fits inside them, not the other way round.

### What goes on the card

The card is what makes the whole thing fast. When the questions are written down, I never spend the first five minutes deciding what to look at.

Mine asks five questions, always in the same order:

| On the card | Why it's there |
| --- | --- |
| What happened this week that I want to remember? | Good weeks blur together without it. |
| What did I promise someone? | Promises are the easiest thing to lose. |
| What is waiting on me? | Someone is often stuck on a two-minute reply. |
| What is coming up in the next two weeks? | One week ahead is often too late to prepare. |
| Which three things would make next week good? | It ends the review with a decision. |

Copy mine to start with, then change one question a month at most. A card that changes every week is just another list to manage.

## The fifteen minutes, step by step

I set a timer, because without one the review quietly grows back into an hour. Then I go through the same five steps in the same order every week.

1. **Empty the landing place (three minutes).** Everything I noted during the week goes onto one list. I don't sort it yet.
2. **Look back at the calendar (three minutes).** I scroll through the past seven days and note anything that needs a follow-up: a thank-you, a reply, a booking.
3. **Look ahead two weeks (four minutes).** Birthdays, deadlines, trips and anything else that needs preparing now rather than the night before.
4. **Answer the card (three minutes).** A line for each question, no more. This is where the week becomes something I can actually see.
5. **Pick three things (two minutes).** Not ten. Three things that, if they happen, make next week a good one.

The last step is the one that pays. Three things is few enough to remember on Monday morning without opening anything. Everything else on the list still exists, but it no longer competes for the front of my mind.

When the timer goes off, I stop, even if I'm in the middle of a step. Finishing on time matters more than finishing perfectly, because it is what makes next Friday's review easy to start.

## What to leave out

Most of the work of a short review is deciding what it isn't. Each of the things below crept in at some point, and each one turned fifteen minutes into forty.

I don't clean up my inbox during the review. I don't reorganise my notes, and I don't plan the next quarter. Those are real jobs that deserve their own slot, but inside the review they are just a comfortable way to avoid answering the card.

I also stopped judging the week. The review is a look at what happened, not a score. If a week went badly, I write one line about why and move on, because a review that makes you feel bad is a review you will start skipping.

## Your first one this week

Don't wait for a fresh start or a new notebook. Put fifteen minutes in your calendar for this Friday, write the five questions on a card, and set a timer.

Expect the first one to feel rushed. That's fine: the second one is faster, and by the fourth it mostly runs on its own.

If you only keep one part, keep the three things. Choosing them on Friday is what makes Monday feel like a continuation instead of a fresh scramble.
