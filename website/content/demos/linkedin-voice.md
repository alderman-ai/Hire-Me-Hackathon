Five LinkedIn posts in Addvery's company voice each met all 21 rules of a voice rubric. An AI judge that wrote none of them scored them blind. The same AI model, asked for a post in Addvery's brand voice without the rubric, met 12.

## One topic, two posts

Why AI pilots stall before production. First, a post written to the rubric. Then the same AI model, asked for "Addvery's brand voice" with Addvery's public tagline and nothing else.

> [!post] Post 1: Why AI pilots stall before production
> format: Text post
> label: Meets 21 of 21 voice rules, 0 AI tells
>
> Production invoices arrive as phone photos, some with a thumb over the total.
>
> The pilot never saw one. So before the next build, we ask for this:
>
> 1. What did last month's inbox look like, unsorted?
> The demo runs on hand-picked PDFs, and the model reads every one. The real inbox has scans at an angle and credit notes. It has a supplier who changed their template in March, and three invoices in one email. We ask for that month, as it arrived.
>
> 2. Whose name is on the exception queue?
> Everything the model can't read lands there. Finance thinks IT owns it. IT thinks the vendor does. The vendor's contract ended with the pilot. We want one name, for the queue and for keeping it running after we leave.
>
> 3. What can it cost per month and still pay back?
> The pilot budget covers the build. Production adds API calls, hosting and about 20 hours a month of someone fixing misreads. Leave those out, and a business case that promised payback in 8 months can land nearer 3 years.
>
> On a typical invoice project, the model is the smallest line on that monthly bill.

> [!post] The generic post
> format: Text post
> label: Meets 12 of 21 voice rules
> variant: contrast
>
> The AI demo went well. The board liked it. That was eight months ago, and it still isn't live.
>
> Plenty of AI pilots end up right here, somewhere between "great proof of concept" and "so who's actually running this?"
>
> From what we see, it's usually one of these five:
>
> 1. The pilot ran on a clean data extract. Production runs on the real systems, with the duplicates, the empty fields and the spreadsheet finance keeps on a shared drive.
>
> 2. Nobody owns it after the demo. The innovation team built it, IT has to run it, the business has to use it, and none of the three has it in next year's budget.
>
> 3. Success was never defined. "Let's see what AI can do" is a good question for a workshop and a bad brief for something that needs sign-off to go live.
>
> 4. The process around it stayed the same. If the model drafts an answer in seconds but approval still takes four people and a PDF, you've sped up the part that wasn't slow.
>
> 5. Security and legal arrive at the end, when the architecture is fixed and the only possible answer is "start again."
>
> Read the list again. The model isn't on it.
>
> So before the next pilot kicks off, we'd ask three questions:
>
> - Who runs this on day one in production, and is it in their budget?
> - Which live system does it read from, and has anyone looked at that data?
> - Which number has to move for us to keep it switched on?
>
> No answers yet? Good. That's where the pilot starts, before anyone picks a model.
>
> Got a pilot that's been "nearly ready" since spring? Message us. We'll tell you straight whether it's worth pushing to production or switching off.
>
> #AI #Digitalisation #DigitalTransformation

The generic post isn't AI slop: it has no stock AI phrases. It misses on the voice. Its opening runs past LinkedIn's "see more" cut mid-sentence, it lists reasons instead of telling a story from the work, and it ends with a sales pitch.

## How it works

1. **Capture.** Addvery's public voice becomes rules: four hard don'ts (no corporate filler, no pep talk, no AI hype, no invented results or real names) and seven voice rules. Ten LinkedIn basics sit underneath, such as a hook that fits above the "see more" cut.
2. **Write.** One AI writer per post, hook first. A script counts line lengths and stock AI phrases before anything is handed in.
3. **Judge.** A separate AI judge scores every post blind against all 21 rules, with the posts relabelled and shuffled and a generic post mixed in. Misses go back for a rewrite.
4. **Sign-off.** A person reads them and decides. AI drafts; people approve.

## The rubric

The judge places each post in one of four tiers.

| Tier | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Unpostable | Breaks a hard rule |
| Postable | Clean, but could be any company |
| Good | Sounds like Addvery |
| Great | Sounds like Addvery, and stops the scroll |

The rubric caught its own writers out. In the first round, all five writers copied the rule sheet's example lines. Each post looked fine alone; side by side they read like a template. So the rubric gained rules for the whole set: no two posts may share an opener, a click line, an aside or an ending.

## How Addvery sounds, in five rules

1. Start with something you could count or photograph.
2. Tell it like the people who did the work: what happened, what broke, what we do now.
3. Short sentences and plain words. No filler, no pep talk.
4. One dry joke, at our own expense or the work's.
5. AI is delivery work: data, owners, cost and sign-off, tied to a number the business runs on.

## The other four posts

Two text posts and two 8-slide carousels, all graded Great.

> [!carousel] Post 2: Fix the data before the model
> format: Document carousel, 8 slides
> label: Meets 21 of 21 voice rules, 0 AI tells
> slides: /demos/linkedin-voice/post-2
>
> The CRM says 12,000 active customers. Finance invoiced 7,400 last year.
>
> That gap decides the project. The slides show where it hides.
>
> Your numbers will differ. The gap is usually there: duplicates, accounts that went quiet years ago, a second customer list in someone's Excel file.
>
> An AI model built on that list treats every duplicate as a real customer.
>
> So for two weeks, nobody builds. That costs less than a pilot that misses its launch date by a quarter. And if the project isn't worth building, you learn that before the budget is spent.

> [!post] Post 3: The launch date, not the deck
> format: Text post
> label: Meets 21 of 21 voice rules, 0 AI tells
>
> Twelve weeks into most strategy projects, the launch date still says "Q3, TBC".
>
> We write a three-line page before any slides. Open the post to see it.
>
> We fill it in during the first two weeks of a strategy project.
>
> 1. The number: one figure the business already reports, like days to close the month.
> 2. The date: the day something goes live for real users.
> 3. The owner: one name inside the company who answers for that date.
>
> Agreeing the number tends to take an hour. The owner line takes longest, because a name next to a date is a real commitment.
>
> We've typed "Q3, TBC" onto slides ourselves. More than once.
>
> The analysis still happens after that. It works backwards from the three lines. A finding that can't move the number by the date goes on the "later" list.

> [!carousel] Post 4: What not to hand to AI yet
> format: Document carousel, 8 slides
> label: Meets 21 of 21 voice rules, 0 AI tells
> slides: /demos/linkedin-voice/post-4
>
> A customer wants 4,800 CZK back. The model has already said yes.
>
> Who signs it? Our answer is further down.
>
> In most pilots, someone reads every suggestion before a customer sees it. That person is your governance. In production, they're gone.
>
> Before go-live, we ask four questions. Who signs off a refund? Who checked the data the model reads? Who fixes a wrong answer? Could you explain the decision to an auditor? Auditors, it turns out, don't count "94% confident" as a signature.
>
> Where the answer is "nobody", that part stays with people for now. One wrong refund rule, repeated a few hundred times, can cost more margin than the pilot saved.
>
> The rest goes to the model gladly: tagging complaints, matching them to orders, reading delivery notes. It's dull and checkable, which is where the hours come back. Start there.

> [!post] Post 5: The spreadsheet that runs the company
> format: Text post
> label: Meets 21 of 21 voice rules, 0 AI tells
>
> Pricing_final_v7_use-this-one.xlsx. One person in the company understands it.
>
> We don't start by replacing it. Read on for what we do instead.
>
> Almost every scale-up we meet has a file like it. It runs pricing, the production plan or the month-end close. It started as a quick fix. Now the business can't close a quarter without it.
>
> 1. Map what it really decides.
> Not the tab names. The cells that set a price, a delivery date, a bonus. Usually three or four tabs decide. The rest copy them.
>
> 2. Find the person who knows the exceptions.
> We sit with them for a day, because none of it is written down. The distributor who always gets 4% off. The macro you run twice in December. The orange cells. (We have yet to meet one that was safe to touch.)
>
> 3. Replace the riskiest part first. Not all of it.
> Usually it's the tab where one wrong cell changes the margin on every quote. That tab moves into a proper system with an owner and a log. The rest stays in Excel for now.
>
> A full rebuild usually takes a year and still misses the exceptions. One tab moves in weeks. Quotes carry the right margin from then on.
>
> Pricing_final_v7_use-this-one.xlsx is still there. It no longer sets the margin.

## What the score can't tell you

The score shows the posts follow the 21 rules. It isn't proof that they sound like Addvery to Addvery's own team: that takes someone from Addvery reading them. The rules come from a short capture of Addvery's public voice, not from posts Addvery graded, so this is a directional check, not a validated one. Every number in the posts is illustrative, never a client result, and nothing was posted to LinkedIn.
