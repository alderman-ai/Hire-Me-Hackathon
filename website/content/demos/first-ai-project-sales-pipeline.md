A lot of companies are saying some version of this: "We want to bring AI in, but we're not going to fire half the team."

Okay, if you say so. But AI only saves you money if it reduces some costs, and the cost it's mostly going to reduce is labor. So if it's not lowering your labor costs, it has to be increasing your revenue.

That's my job as a GTM engineer. **I put AI in your pipeline, so the team you already have brings in more business.** It's how a company keeps its promise that AI is a way to do more business and have the whole team grow together, not to replace half of it.

## Put AI in the pipeline first

If you really want to save your team, the order matters.

> [!quote]
> The first place you need to put AI is in your pipeline, so that you can upskill your people to get more done and at the same time bring in more work. Otherwise you're going to have very uncomfortable decisions, and your comfort levels may vary.
> Alex Alderman, on camera, 2 Nov 2026

In sales, the whole information-gathering process is completely automatable. So I build the tools for the salespeople, or whoever's doing outreach, and teach them how to use them. The meeting prep alone is tons of hours people save on preparing pitches.

## What that looks like when I build it

I come from marketing, the standard path for a GTM engineer, and the job is basically workflows that touch your pipeline. To show what that means, I spent 12 hours building live on camera.

One build was prospect research on a target company: an org chart, a profile of every key decision maker, and, for anyone who's been there less than six months, the job advert they answered. A lot of roles are fuzzy about the responsibilities, so I go looking for the actual advert, in the Wayback Machine or on the job portals, if it's still up somewhere.

The other build was 36 outreach workflows in GetSales: four main customer profiles, three languages (English, Czech and German), three tactics. A classifier decides which lead goes into which one. It needed to be legible more than functional, because no real data runs through it.

## You don't have to pick between personalization and volume

Before AI, reaching out to people meant balancing two things: personalization and volume. You could do heavy research, but that takes a lot of time, so you'd be limited to your key accounts. Or you could shotgun blast lots of people. Most people ended up in the middle or the shotgun blast, and at the time, the shotgun blast was actually correct.

What's new is that you don't have to pick anymore. **Now you can do both.** And this is not known yet.

> [!figure] /demos-assets/if-ai-wont-replace-half-your-team/personalisation-volume.png
> A line from personalization to volume: before AI, one dot in the middle or at the volume end; now both ends are filled, so you can do both
> Before AI you picked a spot on the line. Now you don't have to

The research I did on camera is costly. I can't do that to 30 companies a day. Yet. But once it's automated, it looks costly, and it isn't, right?

Everyone's blasting out AI, low quality, high volume. There's going to be a space for AI so good it looks human: so good they think you spent three days building it just for them, when an agent was running in the background for a couple of hours. That's the niche.

My goal is something that costs one to three or four euros, about 98% automated, where anyone who gets it thinks they're one of your top one or two accounts, because no one else would put that much effort in. **Imagine if you could make everyone in your CRM feel that way.**

## The bottleneck is always human

You can have 20 AI agents running side by side, so time is no longer the bottleneck. The bottleneck is focus, judgment, discernment.

> [!quote]
> The bottleneck is always, always, always human. It's not the amount of stuff that the agents can focus on, it's humans.
> Alex Alderman, on camera, 2 Nov 2026

So the tools come with training. Getting real people to actually use AI is the part you can't automate. The companies that win the next decade won't have the best AI. They'll have the most literate people.

## Someone has to keep refining it

It's still not free: every run costs money. So if it's all built in the first month, why do you still need someone? Because it needs to work better, it needs to get cheaper, people need to learn how to use it, and it needs upgrading, constantly.

> [!video] qrBNOsSYGrQ 597 609
> Built in month one, so why keep me? To make it work better and cheaper, and teach people to use it

A first build starts on the expensive side. I know how to make it more efficient, but a build done in 12 hours under time pressure won't be.

After that, it's looking at the data and the outreach metrics. This approach is doing great, so lean harder into it. That one's not good, so cut it. Make each message cheaper by cutting what's not working and focusing on what is.

Building is a phase, not the deliverable. **Refining is the main work.**

That's how you get to say "we're not going to fire half the team" and mean it.
